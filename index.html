Part A · Starting school

What does “developmentally vulnerable” mean?

Every three years since 2009, teachers fill in a checklist for every child in their first year of full-time school. A child is vulnerable in an area when they score in the bottom 10% band set in 2009, and at risk in the next 15%.

The first year has a different name in every state, but the census checks all of them the same way:

Beyond the vulnerable 1 in 10, another 1 in 7 is at risk

Chapter 2

A decade of progress, reversed

The share of children on track in all five areas rose steadily to a peak in 2018, then fell. Seven of the eight states and territories went backwards in 2024.

Chapter 3

The postcode gap

Children in the most disadvantaged fifth of areas are about 20 points less likely to start school on track than those in the least disadvantaged fifth. The gap has widened since 2009, from 17.1 to 19.9 points.

The gap between rich and poor areas widened in every state

It is widest in the Northern Territory, at 31.1 points.

Since 2021 the gap narrowed slightly, only because children in the least disadvantaged areas fell back (63.7% to 61.1%) faster than the most disadvantaged (42.5% to 41.2%). Nobody improved.

The census stops at the school gate. As the day goes on, a national survey picks up the story.

Chapter 4

The older they get, the less they move

Australia's 24-Hour Movement Guidelines set targets for daily activity, strength activity, screen time and sleep. About 13 in 100 children aged 5–8 meet all of them. By 15–17, fewer than 1 in 100 do.

Fewer than 1 in 100 teenagers meet every guideline

Each figure is one child out of 100. Among girls aged 15–17, none in the sample met them all.

Activity and screen time are where the guidelines break

Sleep holds up reasonably well. By 15–17, fewer than 1 in 10 meet either the activity or the screen guideline.

ReadEach arrow runs from ages 5–8 to 15–17. The further left it points, the more children stop meeting that guideline.

By 15–17, screens take nearly four times as long as activity

At 5–8, children spend about as long being active as on screens. Girls aged 15–17 are the least active of all, at 55 minutes a day.

ReadActivity grows left of the centre line and screens to the right. Dashed lines mark the guidelines.

Phones move into the bedroom in the early teens

Fewer than 3 in 100 children aged 5–8 have a phone in their room. By 15–17, more than 8 in 10 do.

Teenagers sleep worse, not just less

Nearly half of 2–5 year olds slept very well. By 15–17, about 1 in 5 did.

Movement and sleep slip with age. Diet tells a similar story.

Chapter 5

And what they eat

Children's diets carry more saturated fat than recommended at every age: 13–14% of daily energy against a limit of 10%. Free sugars climb with age and reach the World Health Organization's 10% limit by the teens.

Saturated fat is over the limit at every age. Sugar reaches it by the teens.

The shaded zones show where each nutrient goes over its limit. Bars show the 95% margin of error around each survey estimate.

Place

12.5% of children start school vulnerable on two or more areas, the highest on record. The council a child lives in matters more than the state: rates run from 3.1% to over 90%.

Wealth

The on-track gap between the most and least disadvantaged fifth of areas widened from 17.1 to 19.9 points between 2009 and 2024, and grew in every state.

Age

By 15–17, fewer than 1 in 100 children meet all the movement guidelines and screens take nearly four times as long as activity. Saturated fat stays over the limit at every age.

Support that reaches families before school, and keeps teenagers active and rested, works on both halves of the same story.

Long after dark, one screen is still on. More than 8 in 10 teenagers aged 15 to 17 have a smartphone in their bedroom.

About the data

  • Australian Early Development Census 2009–2024, Department of Education. Time series by council, socio-economic quintile and state. aedc.gov.au
  • National Nutrition and Physical Activity Survey 2023, Australian Bureau of Statistics. abs.gov.au
  • Dietary limits: NHMRC Nutrient Reference Values; WHO Guideline: Sugars intake for adults and children, 2015.
  • Boundaries: ABS Local Government Areas 2024, ASGS Edition 3, simplified with mapshaper.

Notes

  • AEDC results are suppressed where fewer than 15 children were assessed; these councils appear grey. "90% or more" is shown at 90%.
  • ABS figures are survey estimates; tooltips show the margin of error or relative standard error.
