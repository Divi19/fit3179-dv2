Starting behind, slowing down

In 2024, more Australian children arrived at school developmentally vulnerable than at any point in six national collections. And the everyday habits that support a growing child — moving, sleeping, eating well — fade the older they get.

Where children start behind

Every three years, teachers score each child in their first year of school across five areas of development: physical health, social skills, emotional maturity, language and thinking, and communication. A child in the bottom tenth of the 2009 national benchmark on an area is called developmentally vulnerable. Nationally, 23.5% of children were vulnerable on at least one area in 2024 — but your council shapes the odds far more than that average suggests.

Panels zoom into the five largest cities, where most children live. Grey = result suppressed.

The map above is dominated by vast remote councils with few children. Counting children rather than rates flips the picture: the largest numbers of vulnerable children live in fast-growing outer-suburban councils such as Brisbane, Moreton Bay and Logan.

Grouping councils into equal-sized grid squares removes the size bias and shows where things changed between 2021 and 2024. Orange squares got worse, blue squares improved. 58 of 92 squares got worse, and those squares are home to 9 in 10 of the children assessed.

The Australian Capital Territory is reported as a single council, so it is not shown here.

A decade of progress, reversed

The share of children on track on all five areas rose steadily to a peak of 55.4% in 2018. By 2024 it had fallen to 52.9%. Seven of the eight states and territories went backwards; only Queensland held steady. The sharpest falls were in emotional maturity and social competence — the skills children need to settle into a classroom.

3

The postcode gap

Children growing up in Australia's most disadvantaged fifth of areas are about 20 points less likely to be on track than children in the least disadvantaged fifth. The gap exists in every state, and is widest in the Northern Territory.

Why the gap narrowed slightly in 2024: children in the least disadvantaged areas fell further (63.7% → 61.1%) than those in the most disadvantaged (42.5% → 41.2%). Nobody improved.

The census stops at school entry. To see what happens next, we follow a national survey of children's movement, screen time, sleep and diet from age 2 to 17.

4

The older they get, the less they move

Australia's 24-Hour Movement Guidelines ask school-age children for at least 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity every day. Among 5–8 year olds, about 3 in 10 manage it. By 15–17, fewer than 1 in 10 do.

Boys and girls start out equally active. By the late teens, girls average 55 minutes a day, 19 fewer than boys.

At the same time, screens move into the bedroom and the last hour before sleep. Teenagers sleep about an hour and a half less than younger children.

5

And what they eat

Free sugars — those added to food and drinks, plus honey and juice — climb with age. Teenagers get 10.1% of their daily energy from them, just over the World Health Organization's limit and higher than adults.

Place shows where to act. Age shows when.

One in four children starts school already behind, and that share is rising. The children most affected are concentrated in remote communities and disadvantaged suburbs. As they grow, fewer of them move, sleep or eat the way the guidelines recommend. Support that reaches families before school, and keeps teenagers active and rested, addresses both halves of the same story.

About the data

  • Australian Early Development Census (AEDC) 2009–2024, Australian Government Department of Education. Public time-series tables by local government area, socio-economic quintile and state. aedc.gov.au
  • National Nutrition and Physical Activity Survey 2023, Australian Bureau of Statistics. Children's physical activity, screen time and sleep (tables 1–2, 4–5, 12) and food and nutrients (table 2). abs.gov.au
  • Local Government Area boundaries 2024, ABS Australian Statistical Geography Standard Edition 3, simplified with mapshaper. abs.gov.au

Notes

  • AEDC results are suppressed where fewer than 15 children were assessed; these councils appear grey. Results reported as "90% or more" are shown at 90%.
  • The two datasets are not linked: the AEDC covers children starting school, the ABS survey a national sample aged 2–17. Very remote areas are excluded from the ABS survey.
